Claims Operations Rebuild

The claims process was losing SonderMind hundreds of thousands of dollars a quarter. Engineering was rebuilding the service, and I became the connection to the people who would use it: I mapped the work across teams, then designed AI code recommendations for providers and an admin portal for RevOps.

context

Claims were losing $100Ks a quarter. Engineering was rebuilding the service.

The existing claims lifecycle was losing the company hundreds of thousands of dollars in revenue each quarter. That loss, the claims backlog, and the workload facing RevOps were already known problems that motivated the rebuild.

~42,000problematic claims in the existing backlog

Engineering was developing Claims Service, including plans for AI-assisted claim creation. I joined the initiative to understand how those capabilities would fit the work of providers and RevOps.

I led the research and experience design.

As the billing team’s sole designer, with no dedicated PM, I took responsibility for understanding those workflows and connecting them to engineering’s plan. Engineering owned the service architecture; I designed the provider and RevOps experiences around it.

I interviewed operational teams, mapped tools and handoffs into a service blueprint, and ran workshops to identify gaps and agree on priorities. This gave the teams a shared view of the work the service needed to support.

One live investigation made the hidden work visible.

I led a presentation for cross-functional stakeholders and invited RevOps to demonstrate a common claim-investigation workflow. Resolving a single claim took a specialist an estimated 5–15 minutes. Seeing the work move across scattered tools made that effort tangible and helped align leadership on the operational problem.

I presented a strategy that connected three layers of the claims lifecycle.

A better queue could help with today’s claims. Preventing avoidable errors and addressing recurring causes would reduce the need to investigate them in the first place.

I framed prevention and resolution as complementary responsibilities and ran a cross-pod session to agree on ownership. The rule engine extended that strategy into learning from recurring issues.

The provider pod owned building and managing prevention, I designed the provider and RevOps experiences, and engineering owned the service architecture and implementation.

resolve
Resolve

Prototype · not shipped

RevOps needed queues
their manager could shape.

RevOps found, investigated and corrected problem claims across a patchwork of tools, and the legacy portal they relied on needed frequent engineering adjustments. Engineering had already started a benefit verification dashboard in Admin Portal v2, and I saw it as the chance to bring all of RevOps’ claim work into one place and reduce that ongoing patchwork.

I started with one failed-claims queue as a deliberately rough concept. I knew it was not quite right, but wanted something concrete to gather feedback quickly. RevOps research clarified the differences: benefit verification and collections needed separate workflows and urgency signals. I revised the design around work streams the manager could curate.

THE RESOLUTION WORK STOPPED

Then the RevOps manager built her own dashboard, and my workspace didn’t ship.

She created it independently with AI assistance. I learned about it in a brief review while my design work was still underway. It met her team’s immediate needs, and she wanted engineering to focus elsewhere.

My resolution work stopped. The overlap exposed a gap in visibility between parallel efforts, even while stakeholder meetings continued.

What I proposed next: governance for employee-built AI tools, so parallel efforts like this one are visible early. It was a separate initiative prompted by the overlap, not a shipped Claims Service capability.

prevent
Prevent

Reached dev handoff

AI code recommendations arrived after providers had chosen codes.

Incorrect billing codes were one of the biggest reasons provider-submitted claims failed. The engineer had already begun to think about prevention in the form of an enrichment agent: it read the provider’s session note and generated billing-code recommendations. I designed how providers could use those recommendations before submission, while the provider pod owned implementation and ongoing management.

The original flow: providers chose every code, without recommendations.

The agent’s first trigger: the signed note triggered enrichment.

The claim flow already had a note quality checker, and enrichment couldn’t run until the note reached its quality threshold. The only trigger the enrichment agent could use was the provider locking and signing the note. By then the claims page had to be filled out too, so the recommendations arrived after they were needed.

I explored changing the workflow before questioning the dependency.

EXPLORATION 01

Split the flow

Complete the note, run enrichment, then review the claim. Then sign and lock.

Tradeoff: more complexity in a recently redesigned workflow.

EXPLORATION 02

Consolidate review

Generate claim information from documentation and review at submission.

Tradeoff: substantial changes to the flow and design system.

Both explorations removed the separate claims tab and folded claim review into signing the note. Part of the complexity came from what was already there. The note quality checker had its own styles and interactions, so any way of working with the enrichment agent had to fit inside that existing pattern. After several explorations, too much would have had to change for a recommendation to show up in the current experience, especially since the provider team had recently delivered a new, well-performing submission flow.

Decision: Keep the original workflow,
and change when the AI runs.

I brought the tradeoffs to the engineering lead and asked whether another source could make recommendations available earlier. He identified session transcripts, available before the claims submission flow loaded. Together, we found a path that preserved the provider team’s recent redesign.

Transcripts let enrichment run before the claims submission flow loaded.

Providers could see the recommendation in time to review and apply it before submitting. The existing flow stayed intact.

But with a tradeoff

~50%

of providers had transcription enabled at the time. A practical starting population for evaluation, with limited initial reach.

The system recommended. The provider decided.

I set a clear interaction boundary: we were not the clinicians, so a suggestion needed to stay distinct from a code the provider had deliberately selected.

learn
Learn

Prioritized for a future quarter

I proposed rules that catch
recurring claim problems.

Payer changes and implementation bugs kept creating new requests for engineering. I proposed a rule engine to find recurring patterns and recommend changes across groups of claims. The RevOps manager would review each proposal before deciding to make a change.

Some rules could use automation tools already in the admin portal; others would need engineering support. Whether the experience would generate an engineering ticket was still unresolved.

RevOps was enthusiastic about the idea, and the director had the team present it to the CFO. The learning layer was prioritized for a future quarter, but had not launched.

The rule engine remained proposed, with no verified implementation.

Teams adopted the prevention–resolution split.

The provider pod took responsibility for implementation and ongoing management of prevention, while I retained design ownership of the RevOps experience. This was a change in how the work was owned, rather than a measured operational improvement.

I was part of a company wide layoff before significant post-launch tracking. I do not have verified changes in claim accuracy, resolution time, RevOps workload, or revenue.

What I would carry into the next initiative

Make parallel work visible

Ask explicitly what teams are building themselves, not only what they need.

Secure a path to delivery

Agree on a small first release, an owner, and engineering capacity.

Establish measurement early

Set baselines and an accountable owner before launch or team changes.

This work belonged to many people. RevOps walked me through their daily work, the provider team carried prevention forward, and the engineers built the service underneath it all.
